Log-Likelihood Gradients: The One Pattern Behind Logistic, Gaussian, and Softmax Losses

Why does binary cross-entropy give $\partial L/\partial z = p-y$, a Gaussian give $\partial L/\partial \mu = (\mu-x)/\sigma^2$, and softmax cross-entropy give $\partial L/\partial z = q-y$? They look like three separate formulas. They are three dialects of one sentence: move the prediction toward what you observed, scaled by the precision of the model.

There is a moment in learning backpropagation when the formulas stop feeling arbitrary. You have already seen $\partial L/\partial z = p-y$ for logistic regression and $\partial L/\partial z_i = q_i-y_i$ for softmax. Then you meet a Gaussian loss and someone writes $\partial L/\partial \mu = (\mu-x)/\sigma^2$. It looks like a different species.

It is not. All three are the gradient of a negative log-likelihood, and all three are saying: the direction of steepest descent is the gap between what the model expected and what actually happened. The differences come from the coordinate system—probability, mean, or logit—and from how much precision the model assigns to its prediction.

In these notes we will build the whole story from the likelihood principle, derive each gradient slowly, compare the three losses side by side, and then implement them in a numerically stable way. There are two interactive labs: one for binary logistic regression and one for a Gaussian. By the end, you should be able to derive all three gradients from scratch and explain why they have the same shape.

The one-sentence preview For a negative log-likelihood, the gradient with respect to the model's natural parameter is expected sufficient statistic minus observed sufficient statistic. For Bernoulli and categorical models that is $p-y$; for a Gaussian mean it is $\mu-x$ (then scaled by $1/\sigma^2$). Everything else is the chain rule.
Section 01 · The objective

1. From Likelihood to Negative Log-Likelihood

A probabilistic model does not output a single answer; it outputs a distribution over possible answers. For a classification problem, $p_\theta(y\mid x)$ is the probability the model assigns to label $y$ given input $x$. For a regression problem, $p_\theta(x\mid \mu,\sigma^2)$ is a density over real-valued targets. The parameter $\theta$ collects everything we are allowed to change: weights, biases, means, variances.

Suppose we observe a dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{x_1,\dots,x_N\}$ drawn independently from the same unknown distribution. The likelihood of the parameters is the probability (or density) the model assigns to the entire dataset:

$$L(\theta) \;=\; p_\theta(x_1,\dots,x_N) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{N} p_\theta(x_i).$$

The product is the mathematical expression of independence: the probability of seeing all the data is the product of the per-example probabilities. In a supervised setting, replace $x_i$ by the pair $(x_i,y_i)$ and read $p_\theta(x_i,y_i)$ as $p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)\,p(x_i)$; the input marginal does not depend on $\theta$, so it contributes a constant and drops out of the gradient.

The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) is the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} \;=\; \arg\max_\theta \; L(\theta) \;=\; \arg\max_\theta \; \prod_{i=1}^{N} p_\theta(x_i).$$

In practice we never maximize the product directly. We maximize its logarithm instead. This is not an approximation: $\log$ is strictly increasing, so it preserves the location of the maximum. It changes the geometry of the objective, but not the answer.

Why the logarithm is the right transformation

There are four reasons, and they all matter:

1. Same maximizer

$\log$ is monotone, so the $\theta$ that maximizes $L$ also maximizes $\log L$. No information about the optimum is lost.

2. Numerical survival

A product of thousands of probabilities underflows to zero in floating point. A sum of their logs stays in a reasonable range.

3. Calculus decomposes

The derivative of a sum is the sum of derivatives. The product rule would couple every example to every other example.

4. Information adds

$\log p$ is negative surprise. The log-likelihood of independent observations is the total surprise, which is additive.

We therefore define the log-likelihood and the negative log-likelihood (NLL):

$$\ell(\theta) \;=\; \log L(\theta) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p_\theta(x_i), \qquad J(\theta) \;=\; -\ell(\theta) \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p_\theta(x_i).$$

Minimizing $J$ is exactly maximizing $\ell$. The sign flip is a convention that turns "maximize likelihood" into the "minimize loss" language of optimization. Most libraries further divide by $N$ and report the mean NLL, so that the loss does not grow simply because the dataset is larger:

$$J_{\text{mean}}(\theta) \;=\; -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p_\theta(x_i).$$

This distinction between sum and mean is not cosmetic. It changes the scale of the gradient by a factor of $N$, and therefore changes the learning rate you need. We will return to it in Section 8.

A concrete MLE: the biased coin

Flip a coin five times and observe three heads. Let $\theta$ be the probability of heads. The likelihood is $\theta^3(1-\theta)^2$, so

$$\ell(\theta) = 3\log\theta + 2\log(1-\theta), \qquad \frac{d\ell}{d\theta} = \frac{3}{\theta} - \frac{2}{1-\theta}.$$

Setting the derivative to zero gives $3(1-\theta)=2\theta$, hence $\hat{\theta}=3/5=0.6$: the empirical frequency of heads. The second derivative is $-3/\theta^2-2/(1-\theta)^2<0$, so this is a maximum. At $\theta=0.5$, the log-likelihood derivative is $3/0.5-2/0.5=2$, so the NLL derivative is $-2$: gradient descent would increase $\theta$ toward the data. This tiny example already contains the whole intuition of "the gradient points toward the observations."

Likelihood is not a probability of $\theta$ $L(\theta)$ is a function of $\theta$ for fixed data. It is not the probability that $\theta$ is true. A Bayesian posterior would multiply the likelihood by a prior, $p(\theta\mid\mathcal{D})\propto p(\theta)L(\theta)$. The MLE is the special case of a flat prior, and L2 regularization will later turn out to be a Gaussian prior. Keep these three objects separate.
Section 02 · The derivative

2. The Score Function: What Differentiating a Log-Likelihood Actually Means

From here on we work in the loss-gradient language: $L(\theta)$ is the scalar objective we minimize, and every derivative below is $\partial L/\partial\theta$. (Section 1 called the same object $J$; the two names are interchangeable.) The likelihood story told us which loss to use. The rest is calculus.

Define the score function of a model at a single observation:

$$s(\theta;x) \;=\; \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x).$$

The score is the gradient of the log-likelihood with respect to the parameters. The NLL gradient is simply its negative:

$$\nabla_\theta L(\theta) \;=\; -\,s(\theta;x).$$

This sign is worth tattooing somewhere: log-likelihood gradients point uphill; NLL gradients point downhill. For a Bernoulli model, the log-likelihood gradient with respect to the logit is $y-p$, and the NLL gradient is $p-y$. Almost every sign error in practice comes from mixing these two.

A sanity check: the score averages to zero

Because $p_\theta$ is a probability distribution, it integrates to one:

$$\int p_\theta(x)\,dx = 1.$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to $\theta$ and use $\nabla_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta$:

$$0 = \int \nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)\,dx = \int p_\theta(x)\,\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)\,dx = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[s(\theta;x)\big].$$

So the score has mean zero under the model's own distribution. This is a powerful debugging identity: if you compute an average log-likelihood gradient over a large sample from the model and it is not close to zero, either your derivative is wrong or your sampling distribution is not the model's. It is the probabilistic analogue of checking that a force field has no net circulation.

The loss-gradient viewpoint

If you prefer to think in terms of loss functions, this is the bridge. The training objective is a scalar loss $L(\theta)$, and every formula below is a chain rule. The loss sees a prediction—a probability $p$, a probability vector $q$, or a mean $\mu$—and the prediction is produced from a logit or parameter by a link. So the gradient factors as:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \eta} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial \text{prediction}}}_{\text{loss derivative}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial \text{prediction}}{\partial \eta}}_{\text{link derivative}}.$$

Here $\eta$ is the model's natural parameter: the logit $z$ for Bernoulli and categorical models, the mean $\mu$ for a Gaussian. For a vector prediction such as $q$, the link derivative is a Jacobian and the product is a sum over classes. The two factors individually look singular or complicated, but for the canonical pairings their product collapses to a simple residual:

Model Prediction Loss derivative wrt prediction Link derivative Result $\partial L/\partial\eta$
Bernoulli $p=\sigma(z)$ $\frac{p-y}{p(1-p)}$ $\frac{\partial p}{\partial z}=p(1-p)$ $p-y$
Gaussian (fixed $\sigma^2$) $\mu$ $\frac{\mu-x}{\sigma^2}$ $1$ (identity link) $(\mu-x)/\sigma^2$
Categorical $q=\operatorname{softmax}(z)$ $-\frac{y_k}{q_k}$ (componentwise) $\frac{\partial q}{\partial z}$ (Jacobian) $q-y$

The Bernoulli row is the most dramatic cancellation: the loss derivative blows up near $p=0$ or $p=1$, but the sigmoid derivative vanishes at exactly the same rate. Their product is bounded and well-behaved. This is not a lucky coincidence; it is a consequence of using the canonical link of an exponential family, which we will unpack in Section 7.

The coordinate matters The simple residual $p-y$ is the gradient with respect to the logit $z$, not with respect to the probability $p$. With respect to $p$, the gradient is $(p-y)/(p(1-p))$; the sigmoid derivative then converts it to $p-y$. The same distinction explains $q-y$: it is the gradient with respect to the logits, after the softmax Jacobian has been applied.

Fisher information: the curvature of surprise

The score also defines the Fisher information, the local curvature of the log-likelihood:

$$\mathcal{I}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[s(\theta;x)s(\theta;x)^\top\big] = -\,\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta^2 \log p_\theta(x)\big].$$

For a Bernoulli logit, $\mathcal{I}(z)=p(1-p)$: the model is most informative near $p=0.5$ and least informative near certainty. For a Gaussian mean with known variance, $\mathcal{I}(\mu)=1/\sigma^2$: a smaller variance means each observation carries more information about the mean. These are exactly the factors that appear in the gradients, which is why the same formulas keep reappearing.

Why this matters for optimization Fisher information tells you how much the loss curves in each direction. A plain gradient step treats every direction as equally important; a natural gradient step preconditions by $\mathcal{I}^{-1}$ so that a step of fixed size in probability space corresponds to a fixed change in likelihood. You do not need natural gradients to use these notes, but this is the reason the curvature of NLL is not just an abstract curiosity.
Section 03 · Bernoulli model

3. Binary Logistic Regression: Deriving $\partial L/\partial z = p-y$

Start with the simplest probabilistic classifier. The label is binary, $y\in\{0,1\}$. The model computes a linear score $z=w^\top x+b$ and turns it into a probability with the sigmoid:

$$p \;=\; P(y=1\mid x) \;=\; \sigma(z) \;=\; \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}, \qquad z \;=\; w^\top x+b.$$

The quantity $z$ is the log-odds or logit:

$$z \;=\; \log\frac{p}{1-p}.$$

This is why $z$ can be any real number while $p$ is trapped in $(0,1)$. A logit of $0$ means odds $1:1$, so $p=0.5$. A logit of $2$ means odds $e^2\approx7.39:1$, so $p\approx0.881$. The sigmoid is the bridge from the unconstrained linear world to the bounded probability world.

The negative log-likelihood of one example

For a Bernoulli random variable, the probability mass function is $p^y(1-p)^{1-y}$. Taking the negative log gives the binary cross-entropy:

$$L(p,y) \;=\; -\Big[\,y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)\,\Big].$$

Check the two cases. If $y=1$, the second term vanishes and $L=-\log p$: the loss is the negative log-probability assigned to the correct class. If $y=0$, the first term vanishes and $L=-\log(1-p)$: the loss is the negative log-probability assigned to the correct class $0$. In both cases, the loss is "negative log probability of what actually happened."

Derivation 1: differentiate through the probability

First differentiate $L$ with respect to $p$:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial p} = -\left(\frac{y}{p} - \frac{1-y}{1-p}\right) = -\frac{y(1-p)-(1-y)p}{p(1-p)} = \frac{p-y}{p(1-p)}.$$

Then differentiate the sigmoid. A useful identity is $\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))=p(1-p)$:

$$\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = p(1-p).$$

Multiply the two factors. The $p(1-p)$ cancels exactly:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial p}\cdot\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = \frac{p-y}{p(1-p)}\cdot p(1-p) = p-y.$$

That cancellation is the heart of the story. The loss derivative is huge near saturation, and the sigmoid derivative is tiny near saturation; their product is the simple, bounded error $p-y$.

Derivation 2: the logit form (the one libraries use)

There is a shorter route that avoids differentiating $\log p$ near zero. Substitute $p=\sigma(z)$ and simplify the log-likelihood before differentiating:

$$y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p) = yz-\log(1+e^z).$$

Let us verify that identity. For $y=1$ it says $\log\sigma(z)=z-\log(1+e^z)$, which follows from $\sigma(z)=e^z/(1+e^z)$. For $y=0$ it says $\log(1-\sigma(z))=-\log(1+e^z)$, which follows from $1-\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^z)$. Therefore

$$L(z,y) \;=\; \log(1+e^z)-yz \;=\; \operatorname{softplus}(z)-yz.$$

Differentiate with respect to $z$. The derivative of $\log(1+e^z)$ is $e^z/(1+e^z)=p$:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \frac{e^z}{1+e^z}-y = p-y.$$

This form is not just prettier; it is the numerically stable form. It never computes $\log(0)$, and it remains accurate when $z$ is very large or very small. We will implement it in Section 8.

From the logit to the weights and bias

Since $z=w^\top x+b$, the chain rule gives the parameter gradients immediately:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = (p-y)\,x, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = p-y.$$

For a batch of $N$ examples, stack the inputs into a matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$, the predictions into a vector $p\in\mathbb{R}^N$, and the labels into $y\in\mathbb{R}^N$. With the mean NLL,

$$\nabla_w J = \frac{1}{N}X^\top(p-y), \qquad \nabla_b J = \frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}^\top(p-y).$$

This is the vectorized form used in every deep learning library. The residual $p-y$ is computed elementwise, then multiplied by the inputs. Notice that the gradient with respect to $w$ is a weighted sum of the input vectors, where the weights are the per-example errors.

What the gradient is telling you

$p-y<0$

The model predicted too little for a positive label. Gradient descent increases $z$, which increases $p$.

$p-y>0$

The model predicted too much for a negative label. Gradient descent decreases $z$, which decreases $p$.

$p-y\approx0$

The prediction is already close to the label. The gradient is small; there is little left to correct.

The residual is bounded: $p-y\in(-1,1)$. That is a feature, not a bug. A wrong and confident prediction produces a gradient close to $-1$ or $+1$, not an infinite one. A correct and confident prediction produces a gradient close to zero. The model spends its effort where it is wrong, but never explodes.

Why not just use squared error with a sigmoid?

Suppose you trained the same sigmoid with the squared error $M=\tfrac12(p-y)^2$. Its gradient with respect to $z$ is

$$\frac{\partial M}{\partial z} = (p-y)\,\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = (p-y)\,p(1-p).$$

The extra factor $p(1-p)$ is disastrous when the model is confidently wrong. Take $y=1$ and $p=0.01$. The BCE gradient is $p-y=-0.99$, a strong signal to increase $z$. The squared-error gradient is $(-0.99)(0.01)(0.99)\approx-0.0098$, roughly one hundred times smaller. The model is maximally wrong, but the gradient has almost vanished because the sigmoid is saturated.

The saturation trap MSE with a sigmoid is not convex in $z$, and its gradient vanishes exactly where you most need it. BCE with a logit is convex in $z$ and its gradient is bounded away from zero when the prediction is wrong. This is the practical reason logistic regression is trained with cross-entropy, not squared error. The same logic explains why softmax is paired with cross-entropy rather than MSE on the probabilities.

Worked example: the numbers from the prompt

Take a single example with label $y=1$ and logit $z=0$. Then

Step 1: probability
$$p=\sigma(0)=\frac{1}{1+e^0}=\frac12=0.5.$$
Step 2: negative log-likelihood
$$L=-\big[y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)\big]=-\log(0.5)=\log 2\approx0.693147.$$
Step 3: gradient with respect to the logit
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}=p-y=0.5-1=-0.5.$$

The negative sign means gradient descent should increase $z$. The model assigned only $50\%$ probability to the correct label, so the logit needs to move upward. If $x=1$, $w=0$, $b=0$, and the learning rate is $1$, one step gives $w\leftarrow0-1(-0.5)(1)=0.5$ and $b\leftarrow0-1(-0.5)=0.5$. The new logit is $z=1$, the new probability is $\sigma(1)\approx0.731$, and the new loss is $-\log(0.731)\approx0.313$, down from $0.693$. The gradient did exactly what it promised.

A full numerical example with features

Now use the values from the interactive lab: $x=1.5$, $w=0.8$, $b=-0.5$, $y=1$. The forward pass is

$$z=wx+b=(0.8)(1.5)-0.5=0.7, \qquad p=\sigma(0.7)\approx0.6682.$$

The loss is $L=-\log(0.6682)\approx0.4032$. The gradients are

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}=0.6682-1=-0.3318, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=(-0.3318)(1.5)=-0.4977, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=-0.3318.$$

With a learning rate of $0.5$, the updates are $w\leftarrow0.8-0.5(-0.4977)=1.0489$ and $b\leftarrow-0.5-0.5(-0.3318)=-0.3341$. The new logit is $1.2392$, the new probability is about $0.7754$, and the new loss is about $0.2543$. The model has become more confident in the correct label, and the loss has fallen.

If your labels are $\{-1,+1\}$ instead of $\{0,1\}$ The NLL is $\log(1+e^{-yz})$, and the gradient with respect to $z$ is $-y\,\sigma(-yz)$. For $y=+1$ this is $p-1$, and for $y=-1$ this is $p$. It is the same formula in a different label convention, so do not mix conventions inside one loss function.

Section 05 · Gaussian model

5. The Gaussian: Why the Gradient Is $(\mu-x)/\sigma^2$

Now switch from a binary label to a real-valued target. The Gaussian model says the observation is the mean plus noise:

$$x \;=\; \mu+\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2), \qquad p(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).$$

The mean $\mu$ is the model's prediction. The variance $\sigma^2$ says how much uncertainty surrounds that prediction. Taking the log turns the exponential into a quadratic:

$$\log p(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2) = -\frac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.$$

The NLL is the negative of this expression:

$$L(\mu,\sigma^2) = \frac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)+\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.$$

The first term does not depend on $\mu$. It is a constant with respect to the mean, so differentiating with respect to $\mu$ only touches the squared residual:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mu} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\left[\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right] = \frac{2(x-\mu)(-1)}{2\sigma^2} = \frac{\mu-x}{\sigma^2}.$$
$$\boxed{\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mu}=\frac{\mu-x}{\sigma^2}\;}$$

The sign is exactly what intuition demands. If the observation $x$ is larger than the prediction $\mu$, the gradient is negative, so gradient descent increases $\mu$ toward $x$. If $x$ is smaller, the gradient is positive, so $\mu$ decreases. The residual $\mu-x$ is the Gaussian analogue of $p-y$.

The MLE for the mean is the sample mean

For $N$ independent observations, sum the gradients and set them to zero:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mu-x_i}{\sigma^2}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{\mu}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_i.$$

The maximum likelihood estimate of the mean is the sample mean. This is the first place where a familiar statistic falls out of a likelihood calculation. The second derivative with respect to $\mu$ is $1/\sigma^2>0$, so the NLL is convex in $\mu$ and this stationary point is the unique minimum.

Fixed variance: Gaussian NLL is precision-weighted MSE

If $\sigma^2$ is fixed, the first term is a constant and the NLL is

$$L(\mu) \;=\; \text{const}+\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.$$

Minimizing this is exactly minimizing squared error. The factor $1/\sigma^2$ does not change the location of the minimum, but it does change the scale of the gradient. A small variance means the model is very confident, so each residual produces a large gradient; a large variance means the model is uncertain, so the same residual produces a smaller gradient. This is why Gaussian NLL is often described as a precision-weighted squared error.

Learning-rate consequence If you compare the gradient of $\tfrac12(x-\mu)^2$, which is $\mu-x$, with the Gaussian NLL gradient $(\mu-x)/\sigma^2$, you will see that the Gaussian version is the MSE gradient multiplied by $1/\sigma^2$. For fixed $\sigma^2$, this is equivalent to rescaling the learning rate by $1/\sigma^2$. If you change the assumed noise level, you must retune the step size unless you account for this factor.

Learning the variance as well

If $\sigma^2$ is also a parameter, differentiate the NLL with respect to it:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \sigma^2} = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^4} = \frac{\sigma^2-(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^4}.$$

Setting the sum over $N$ examples to zero gives

$$\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{MLE}}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-\hat{\mu})^2.$$

Notice the denominator is $N$, not $N-1$. The MLE is biased downward; the unbiased sample variance uses $N-1$. This is a small but important distinction. It also explains why a single observation gives a degenerate MLE $\hat{\sigma}^2=0$ when the mean is free to match the point: the likelihood can be made arbitrarily large by collapsing the variance around a perfect fit. With $N\ge2$ and a fixed mean, the average squared residual is the MLE.

The log-variance term is essential. Without it, increasing $\sigma^2$ would always reduce the squared-residual term, and the model would drive $\sigma^2\to\infty$ (infinite uncertainty). The $\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$ term penalizes variances that are too large, while the squared-residual term penalizes variances that are too small. Their balance produces a sensible estimate.

In code, we usually parameterize the standard deviation as $\sigma=e^{s}$ or the variance as $\sigma^2=e^{v}$ so that positivity is automatic. Then the chain rule adds a factor:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \sigma} = -\frac{1}{\sigma}+\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{\sigma^3}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial s} = \sigma\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial \sigma}.$$

The multivariate Gaussian: whitened residuals

For a $d$-dimensional observation with mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$,

$$\log p(x\mid\mu,\Sigma) = -\frac d2\log(2\pi)-\frac12\log|\Sigma| -\frac12(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu).$$

Differentiating with respect to the mean gives

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mu}=\Sigma^{-1}(\mu-x).$$

When $\Sigma=\sigma^2 I$, this reduces to $(\mu-x)/\sigma^2$. In general, $\Sigma^{-1}$ rotates and rescales the residual so that directions with small variance receive larger gradients. This is the Mahalanobis geometry of the Gaussian, and it is the reason whitening transformations are useful in optimization.

Worked example: $x=5$, $\mu=3$, $\sigma=1.5$

Step 1: variance and residual
$$\sigma^2=2.25,\qquad x-\mu=2,\qquad (x-\mu)^2=4.$$
Step 2: log density and NLL
$$\log p=-\frac12\log(2\pi\cdot2.25)-\frac{4}{2\cdot2.25} \approx-1.3244-0.8889=-2.2133, \qquad L\approx2.2133.$$
Step 3: gradients
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mu}=\frac{3-5}{2.25}\approx-0.8889, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial\sigma^2} =\frac{2.25-4}{2(2.25)^2}\approx-0.1728.$$

The mean gradient is negative, so gradient descent moves $\mu$ upward toward $5$. The variance gradient is negative, so gradient descent increases $\sigma^2$: the residual is larger than the current variance, so the model should become less confident. Both signs match the story.

Section 06 · Categorical model

6. Softmax Cross-Entropy: The Categorical Version of the Same Story

A classifier with $K$ classes outputs a vector of logits $z\in\mathbb{R}^K$. Softmax turns it into a probability vector $q$:

$$q_i=\operatorname{softmax}(z)_i=\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{z_j}}, \qquad i=1,\dots,K.$$

The label is a one-hot vector $y\in\{0,1\}^K$ with exactly one entry equal to $1$. If the true class is $c$, then $y_c=1$ and all other entries are zero. The categorical negative log-likelihood is

$$L(z,y)=-\sum_{i=1}^{K}y_i\log q_i=-\log q_c.$$

This is exactly the cross-entropy between the one-hot label distribution and the model's distribution. Because the label is one-hot, only the true class contributes to the loss. The model is punished by the negative log-probability it assigned to the correct answer.

The log-sum-exp form

Substitute the softmax definition and simplify:

$$L(z,y) =-\log\frac{e^{z_c}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} =-z_c+\log\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{z_j} =-z_y+\operatorname{LSE}(z),$$

where $\operatorname{LSE}(z)=\log\sum_j e^{z_j}$ is the log-sum-exp function. This is the categorical analogue of the logistic form $\log(1+e^z)-yz$. In both cases, the loss is a linear term in the correct logit minus a smooth normalizer. Differentiating the normalizer is the whole game.

Derivation: differentiate the normalizer

The derivative of log-sum-exp is a softmax:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial z_i}\log\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{z_j} =\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} =q_i.$$

The derivative of $-z_y$ with respect to $z_i$ is $-y_i$ (it is $-1$ when $i$ is the true class, and $0$ otherwise). Therefore

$$\boxed{\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}=q_i-y_i\;}$$

In vector form, $\partial L/\partial z=q-y$. This is the same "prediction minus target" residual as logistic regression, now with one residual per class.

The Jacobian route (and why the cancellation is not an accident)

If you want to see the full chain rule, differentiate the softmax itself. For $i,j\in\{1,\dots,K\}$,

$$\frac{\partial q_k}{\partial z_i}=q_k(\delta_{ki}-q_i),$$

where $\delta_{ki}=1$ if $k=i$ and $0$ otherwise. Then

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} =\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k}\frac{\partial q_k}{\partial z_i} =\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(-\frac{y_k}{q_k}\right)q_k(\delta_{ki}-q_i) =-y_i+q_i\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_k =q_i-y_i.$$

The last step uses $\sum_k y_k=1$, which is true because $y$ is one-hot. The $1/q_k$ from the log cancels the $q_k$ from the softmax Jacobian, leaving the residual. This is the multivariate version of the sigmoid cancellation.

Properties worth remembering

The gradient sums to zero

$\sum_i(q_i-y_i)=\sum_i q_i-\sum_i y_i=1-1=0$. This reflects softmax shift invariance: adding a constant to every logit changes nothing, so the gradient has no component in the all-ones direction.

The true class is pushed up

For the true class $c$, $q_c-1<0$, so gradient descent increases $z_c$. For every other class, $q_i-0>0$, so gradient descent decreases $z_i$. The gap between the true logit and the rest grows.

The gradient is bounded

Each component lies in $(-1,1)$. Even if the model is confidently wrong, the gradient does not explode. The loss may be large, but the update direction remains well-behaved.

Binary logistic is the special case $K=2$

With logits $[z_0,z_1]$, $q_1=\sigma(z_1-z_0)$. The gradient with respect to $z_1$ is $q_1-y_1$, and with respect to $z_0$ is $-(q_1-y_1)$, which is the logistic gradient with $z=z_1-z_0$.

Worked example: three classes

Let $z=[1,2,0]$ and let the true class be class $2$ (zero-indexed, so the third class). The exponentials are $e^1\approx2.7183$, $e^2\approx7.3891$, and $e^0=1$. Their sum is $11.1073$, so

$$q\approx[0.2447,\,0.6652,\,0.0900].$$

The loss is $L=-\log(0.0900)\approx2.4076$. The gradient is

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}=q-y \approx[0.2447,\,0.6652,\,0.0900]-[0,0,1] =[0.2447,\,0.6652,\,-0.9100].$$

The true class has a negative gradient, so its logit rises. The two wrong classes have positive gradients, so their logits fall. The components sum to approximately zero, as they must. If the model were perfect, $q=y$ and the gradient would be exactly zero.

Label smoothing and confidence A one-hot target asks the model to drive $q_c$ all the way to $1$, which requires $z_c\to\infty$. Label smoothing replaces $y$ with $y^\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)y+\varepsilon/K$. The gradient becomes $q-y^\varepsilon$, so the model stops pushing once $q_c\approx1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/K$. This caps confidence, improves calibration, and prevents the loss from diverging when the true class is extremely unlikely under the current model. The same formula applies to sample weights, as long as you keep track of the normalization.

For a neural network, the logits are produced by a final linear layer, $z=Wh+b$. The gradient with respect to $W$ is the outer product of the residual and the hidden activations:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}=(q-y)\,h^\top, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=q-y.$$

For a batch, average the outer products. This is exactly the same structure as the logistic case, with the scalar residual $p-y$ replaced by the vector residual $q-y$. Part II of these notes derives the softmax Jacobian, temperature scaling, and the log-sum-exp trick in more detail; here the goal is to see it as one more log-likelihood gradient.

Section 07 · The pattern

7. The Unified View: Observed Minus Expected

We have derived three gradients that look different but behave the same way. Here they are side by side:

Model Log-likelihood $\log p$ NLL gradient Parameter gradient
Bernoulli
$p=\sigma(z)$		 $yz-\log(1+e^z)$ $p-y$ $(p-y)x$ for $w$; $p-y$ for $b$
Gaussian
fixed $\sigma^2$		 $-\frac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$ $(\mu-x)/\sigma^2$ $(\mu-x)/\sigma^2$ for $\mu$
Categorical
$q=\operatorname{softmax}(z)$		 $z_y-\log\sum_j e^{z_j}$ $q-y$ $(q-y)h^\top$ for $W$; $q-y$ for $b$

In every row, the gradient is a residual: a prediction minus a target, or a target minus a prediction. The sign depends on whether you differentiated the log-likelihood or the NLL, and on which parameter you chose. The magnitude depends on the model's precision. The structure is universal.

Why the pattern repeats: exponential families

The three models are all members of the exponential family. A member of this family can be written as

$$p(x\mid\eta)=h(x)\exp\!\big(\eta^\top T(x)-A(\eta)\big),$$

where $\eta$ is the natural parameter, $T(x)$ is a sufficient statistic, $A(\eta)$ is the log-partition function, and $h(x)$ is a base measure that does not depend on $\eta$. Taking the log gives

$$\log p(x\mid\eta)=\eta^\top T(x)-A(\eta)+\log h(x).$$

Differentiate with respect to $\eta$. The term $\log h(x)$ disappears, and the derivative of $A(\eta)$ is the expected sufficient statistic:

$$\nabla_\eta\log p(x\mid\eta)=T(x)-\nabla_\eta A(\eta) =T(x)-\mathbb{E}_{p}[T(X)].$$
$$\boxed{\;\nabla_\eta\,\text{NLL}=\mathbb{E}_{p}[T(X)]-T(x)\;}$$

This is the sentence from the beginning of the notes, now proved: the NLL gradient is expected minus observed. The model compares what it expected to see with what it actually saw, and moves its parameters to close the gap.

Bernoulli

$T(x)=x$, $\eta=z$, $\mathbb{E}[T]=p$. NLL gradient $p-y$. The observed statistic is the label; the expected statistic is the predicted probability.

Gaussian

$T(x)=x$, $\eta=\mu/\sigma^2$, $\mathbb{E}[T]=\mu$. NLL gradient $\mu-x$ in natural coordinates; chain rule by $1/\sigma^2$ gives $(\mu-x)/\sigma^2$.

Categorical

$T(x)$ is the one-hot vector, $\eta=z$, $\mathbb{E}[T]=q$. NLL gradient $q-y$. The observed statistic is the one-hot label; the expected statistic is the softmax vector.

The log-partition function $A(\eta)$ is the cumulant generating function of $T(X)$: its gradient is the mean, and its Hessian is the covariance. That Hessian is the Fisher information. This is why the curvature of the NLL and the variance of the score are the same object. For Bernoulli, $A(z)=\log(1+e^z)$, $A'(z)=p$, and $A''(z)=p(1-p)$. For categorical, $A(z)=\log\sum_j e^{z_j}$, $\nabla A=q$, and $\nabla^2A=\operatorname{diag}(q)-qq^\top$. The algebra keeps folding back on itself because it is all one family.

Backpropagation: where the cancellation happens

A computation graph for logistic regression looks like this:

$x,w,b$ → $z=w^\top x+b$ → $p=\sigma(z)$ → $L=\operatorname{BCE}(p,y)$

Backprop computes the upstream gradient $\partial L/\partial p$, multiplies by the local sigmoid derivative $\partial p/\partial z=p(1-p)$, and passes the result to the linear layer. The intermediate factors can be large and small, but their product is $p-y$. A naive implementation that stores $\partial L/\partial p$ and then multiplies by a numerically zero $p(1-p)$ loses precision; a fused implementation computes $p-y$ directly from the logit.

Node Local derivative What it does
Sigmoid $\partial p/\partial z=p(1-p)$ Shrinks the gradient near saturation.
BCE $\partial L/\partial p=(p-y)/(p(1-p))$ Grows the gradient near saturation.
Product $\partial L/\partial z=p-y$ The two singularities cancel exactly.
Softmax $\partial q_k/\partial z_i=q_k(\delta_{ki}-q_i)$ Couples all classes through the normalizer.
CE + softmax $\partial L/\partial z_i=q_i-y_i$ The Jacobian and the $1/q$ cancel.
Gaussian mean $\partial L/\partial\mu=(\mu-x)/\sigma^2$ No link nonlinearity; the precision sets the scale.

Regularization is a prior in disguise

The likelihood story has a Bayesian sequel. If you add an L2 penalty $\lambda\|w\|^2/2$ to the NLL, you are doing maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation with a Gaussian prior $w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/\lambda)$. The gradient gains a term $\lambda w$, which pulls the weights toward zero. Weight decay is not an ad hoc trick; it is the log-prior of a zero-mean Gaussian. L1 regularization corresponds to a Laplace prior and produces sparsity. This is another place where "write the log-probability, then differentiate" pays off.

What to remember from the unification If you can write $\log p$ for a model, you can derive its training gradient. The exponential-family pattern tells you what to expect: a residual between the observed sufficient statistic and its expected value. The chain rule then converts that residual into gradients for whatever parameters you actually use. You do not need to memorize three separate formulas; you need to remember one pattern and the Jacobian of each link.
Section 08 · Implementation

8. Numerical Stability: Compute the Gradient Without Blowing Up

The formulas are exact in real arithmetic. In floating point, the naive versions can overflow, underflow, or produce $0\cdot\infty$. The fix is not to clip aggressively; it is to rewrite the same mathematical object in a form that never evaluates a huge exponential or a logarithm of zero.

Stable binary cross-entropy with logits

The unstable version computes $p=\sigma(z)$ and then $\log p$ or $\log(1-p)$. When $z=1000$, $p$ rounds to $1$, and $\log(1-p)$ becomes $\log(0)=-\infty$. When $z=-1000$, the mirror image happens. The stable version uses the softplus identity:

$$\log(1+e^z)=\max(z,0)+\log\!\left(1+e^{-|z|}\right).$$

Therefore

$$L(z,y)=\max(z,0)-yz+\log\!\left(1+e^{-|z|}\right).$$

The exponent is now always negative or zero, so $e^{-|z|}\in(0,1]$ and the logarithm is finite. At $z=1000$, $y=1$, the first two terms cancel to zero and the last term is negligible; the loss is $0$, as it should be. At $z=1000$, $y=0$, the loss is $1000$, which is large but finite and correct.

The gradient is still $p-y$. To compute $p$ stably, use the piecewise sigmoid:

# Stable sigmoid: never exponentiate a large positive number def sigmoid(z): if z >= 0: return 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-z)) ez = math.exp(z) return ez / (1.0 + ez) # Stable BCE with logits def bce_with_logits(z, y): return max(z, 0.0) - y * z + math.log1p(math.exp(-abs(z)))

In PyTorch, use the fused functions. They take logits, not probabilities, and they compute the stable form internally:

# z: raw logits, shape (N,) # y: float labels in {0, 1}, shape (N,) loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(z, y) # K-class logits, integer labels # logits: shape (N, K), targets: shape (N,) loss = F.cross_entropy(logits, targets)
Do not do this
p = torch.sigmoid(z)
loss = -(y * torch.log(p) + (1 - y) * torch.log(1 - p))

It looks like the textbook formula, but it produces NaNs when the model is confident and wrong—exactly the case you care about. Clipping $p$ to $[10^{-7},1-10^{-7}]$ avoids the NaN but distorts the gradient and hides saturation. Use the logit form instead.

Stable log-softmax

Softmax is shift-invariant, so subtract the maximum logit before exponentiating:

$$\operatorname{log\_softmax}(z)_i =z_i-m-\log\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{z_j-m}, \qquad m=\max_j z_j.$$

Every exponent is now $\le0$, so there is no overflow. The cross-entropy is simply the negative log-softmax at the true class:

$$L=-\operatorname{log\_softmax}(z)_y.$$

If you compute softmax first and then take the log, you lose the stability and may hit $\log(0)$. If you compute cross-entropy from probabilities, you also lose the fused gradient. Keep the logits.

Stable Gaussian NLL

Parameterize the standard deviation as $\sigma=e^{s}$ so that it is always positive. Then

# mu, log_sigma: model outputs; x: target sigma = torch.exp(log_sigma) nll = 0.5 * ((x - mu) / sigma) ** 2 + log_sigma + 0.5 * math.log(2 * math.pi)

The residual is divided by $\sigma$ before squaring, which is safer than squaring a huge residual and then dividing by a tiny variance. The $\log\sigma$ term is the log-Jacobian of the positive parameterization; it is not optional. If you parameterize $\sigma^2$ directly, use a softplus or an exponential and apply the chain rule.

Reduction, batching, and gradient checking

The choice between sum and mean changes the gradient scale. If $J_{\text{sum}}=\sum_i L_i$, then $\nabla J_{\text{sum}}=\sum_i\nabla L_i$. If $J_{\text{mean}}=J_{\text{sum}}/N$, then $\nabla J_{\text{mean}}=\nabla J_{\text{sum}}/N$. A learning rate tuned for a sum will be wrong for a mean by a factor of $N$. The same issue appears with class weights and masks: decide whether you are normalizing by the number of examples or the sum of weights, and be consistent between the forward and backward passes.

For gradient checking, use a central finite difference in double precision:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta} \approx\frac{L(\theta+\epsilon)-L(\theta-\epsilon)}{2\epsilon}, \qquad \epsilon\approx10^{-6}.$$

Compare the relative error, not the absolute error. A relative error below $10^{-5}$ is usually fine for a well-conditioned check; near saturation or with very small probabilities, expect larger errors. Remember that the finite difference checks the NLL gradient, so its sign is opposite to the log-likelihood gradient.

Common pitfalls

Symptom Likely cause Fix
Gradient has the wrong sign Differentiated log-likelihood instead of NLL Log-likelihood: $y-p$. NLL: $p-y$.
Loss is NaN at large logits $\log(\sigma(z))$ or $\log(\operatorname{softmax}(z))$ Use binary_cross_entropy_with_logits or cross_entropy.
Learning rate suddenly wrong Switched from sum to mean reduction, or changed $\sigma^2$ Rescale the learning rate or normalize the loss.
Model learns slowly when confidently wrong MSE with a sigmoid or softmax Use cross-entropy / NLL with logits.
Variance collapses to zero Learned Gaussian without a log-variance term or regularization Use $\log\sigma$ and a proper prior; check for degenerate data.
Softmax gradient does not sum to zero Numerical error or a missing normalization Check that $q$ sums to one and $y$ is one-hot.
Gradient check fails only near saturation Finite-difference step too large relative to curvature Use double precision and a smaller $\epsilon$; check relative error.
Section 09 · Practice

9. Practice: Derive, Compute, and Debug

Work these with a pencil before revealing the solutions. The goal is not to plug into formulas, but to practice the pattern: write the log-likelihood, differentiate, and interpret the sign.

Practice 1 of 6 · Logistic gradient

A logistic model has $x=2$, $w=-1$, $b=0.5$, and label $y=0$. Compute $\partial L/\partial w$ for the single-example NLL. Use natural logarithms.

Hint: First compute $z=wx+b$, then $p=\sigma(z)$. The chain is $\partial L/\partial w=(\partial L/\partial z)(\partial z/\partial w)=(p-y)x$.
Solution: $z=(-1)(2)+0.5=-1.5$. Then $p=\sigma(-1.5)=1/(1+e^{1.5})\approx0.1824$. The logit gradient is $p-y=0.1824-0=0.1824$. Finally, $\partial L/\partial w=(0.1824)(2)\approx0.3649$. The positive sign means gradient descent should decrease $w$; decreasing $w$ makes $z$ more negative and pushes $p$ closer to the correct label $0$.
Practice 2 of 6 · Conceptual

A logistic model has $p=0.01$ and $y=1$. Which statement best explains why BCE produces a much larger gradient than squared error with a sigmoid?

Hint: Write both gradients with respect to the logit. BCE gives $p-y$. MSE gives $(p-y)p(1-p)$. At $p=0.01$, the extra factor is small.
Answer: (a). The sigmoid is saturated near $p=0$, so its derivative $p(1-p)$ is tiny. MSE multiplies by that tiny factor and loses the error signal. BCE's log cancels the saturation and leaves the bounded residual $p-y$.
Practice 3 of 6 · Gaussian gradient

A Gaussian model has observation $x=5$, mean $\mu=3$, and standard deviation $\sigma=2$. Compute $\partial L/\partial\mu$ for the NLL.

Hint: $\partial L/\partial\mu=(\mu-x)/\sigma^2$. The variance is $\sigma^2=4$, not $2$.
Solution: $\sigma^2=4$, so $\partial L/\partial\mu=(3-5)/4=-0.5$. The negative sign means gradient descent increases $\mu$ toward the observation $5$. For reference, the NLL is $\tfrac12\log(8\pi)+4/8\approx2.112$.
Practice 4 of 6 · Softmax gradient

A 3-class softmax model has logits $z=[1,2,0]$ and true class $2$ (zero-indexed). Compute $\partial L/\partial z_2$.

Hint: Compute $q=\operatorname{softmax}(z)$ first. The true class has $y_2=1$, so $\partial L/\partial z_2=q_2-1$.
Solution: The exponentials are $[e, e^2, 1]\approx[2.718,7.389,1]$, with sum $11.107$. Thus $q_2\approx1/11.107\approx0.0900$. Then $\partial L/\partial z_2=q_2-1\approx-0.9100$. The full gradient is approximately $[0.2447,0.6652,-0.9100]$, which sums to zero.
Practice 5 of 6 · Batch gradient

Three logistic examples have predictions $p=[0.9,0.2,0.6]$, labels $y=[1,0,1]$, and inputs $x=[1,2,-1]$. For the mean NLL, compute $\partial J/\partial w$.

Hint: Compute the residual vector $p-y$, multiply elementwise by $x$, then average over the three examples: $\partial J/\partial w=\frac1N\sum_i(p_i-y_i)x_i$.
Solution: $p-y=[-0.1,0.2,-0.4]$. Multiply by $x$: $[-0.1,0.4,0.4]$. Average: $(-0.1+0.4+0.4)/3=0.7/3\approx0.2333$. The positive gradient means gradient descent should decrease $w$. The mean NLL itself is $\frac13[-\log(0.9)-\log(0.8)-\log(0.6)]\approx0.2798$.
Practice 6 of 6 · Implementation

Which expression is the numerically stable way to compute binary cross-entropy from a logit $z$ and label $y\in\{0,1\}$?

Hint: Use the identity $\log(1+e^z)=\max(z,0)+\log(1+e^{-|z|})$. The exponent should never be a large positive number.
