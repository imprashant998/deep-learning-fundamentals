Measuring Surprise and Mismatch: An Intuitive Guide to Information, Entropy, and Cross-Entropy

Why do we measure the gap between two probability distributions with negative logarithms instead of squared error? A visual, step-by-step walkthrough from colored urns and binary prefix codes to constrained optimization.

Source Lecture: CS7015 Deep Learning, Lec 4.10
Instructor: Prof. Mitesh M. Khapra (IIT Madras)
Expository Style: Christopher Olah (Visual Information Theory)

There is a special kind of satisfaction when a formula you have used a hundred times in machine learning suddenly clicks into place—not as an arbitrary algebraic rule to memorize for backpropagation, but as the only natural answer to a simple human question.

Cross-entropy loss, $H(P, Q) = -\sum_i P_i \log Q_i$, is one of those formulas. We reach for it almost reflexively whenever we train a neural network classifier. Yet if you step back and ask why we measure the discrepancy between two probability distributions using negative logarithms rather than simple squared differences, the trail leads us straight into the heart of human surprise, binary communication codes, and the geometry of the probability simplex.

Let's walk down that trail from scratch, building every piece of math out of concrete thought experiments before writing down a single logarithm.

1. Comparing Two Distributions Requires More Than Simple Vector Distance

Imagine you have a large glass urn filled with thousands of colored balls—some Red, some Blue, and some White 00:00:38. Because you packed the urn yourself, you know the exact proportions inside. Formally, drawing a ball is a discrete random variable $X \in \{\text{Red}, \text{Blue}, \text{White}\}$, and you know its true probability distribution $P$:

$$P = \big[P_{\text{Red}},\, P_{\text{Blue}},\, P_{\text{White}}\big] = \big[0.25,\, 0.35,\, 0.40\big]$$

Now suppose a friend walks into the room 00:01:31. You don't let them dump the balls out and count them; they can only peer through the glass, swirl the urn around a bit, and make a guesstimate. They spot quite a few red and blue balls near the top, miss some of the white ones buried at the bottom, and hand you their estimated distribution $Q$ 00:02:06:

$$Q = \big[Q_{\text{Red}},\, Q_{\text{Blue}},\, Q_{\text{White}}\big] = \big[0.35,\, 0.45,\, 0.20\big]$$
Outcome ($X$) True Distribution ($P_i$) Friend's Estimate ($Q_i$) Signed Residual ($P_i - Q_i$) Squared Error $(P_i - Q_i)^2$
Red $0.25$ $0.35$ $-0.10$ $0.0100$
Blue $0.35$ $0.45$ $-0.10$ $0.0100$
White $0.40$ $0.20$ $+0.20$ $0.0400$
Total / Sum $1.00$ $1.00$ $0.00$ $0.0600$

Your friend knows their quick glance wasn't exact, so they ask a natural question: "Tell me, how wrong was I?" 00:02:45

If we treat $P$ and $Q$ as ordinary three-dimensional vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, our first instinct is to compute the squared error between their components 00:03:14:

$$\text{Squared Error}(P, Q) = \sum_{i=1}^{3} (P_i - Q_i)^2 = (-0.10)^2 + (-0.10)^2 + (0.20)^2 = 0.06$$

Squared error is simple, but it ignores the fact that $P$ and $Q$ are not arbitrary vectors—they are probability distributions governed by strict geometric invariants 00:03:35:

  • Non-negativity ($P_i \ge 0$): Probabilities can never be negative.
  • Simplex conservation ($\sum_{i=1}^k P_i = 1$): Because the total mass is locked at $1$, the coordinates are coupled—overestimating Red and Blue forced our friend to underestimate White.

Section 02 · 00:04:17 – 00:10:38

2. Deterministic Labels Are Simply One-Hot Probability Distributions

At first glance, supervised classification doesn't look like our glass urn problem. When we train an image classifier, each training example has a single, unambiguous ground-truth label—not a blend of probabilities. How do we express a certain outcome as a probability distribution?

Consider a sports tournament where four teams—A, B, C, and D—reach the semifinals 00:04:31. Suppose you stopped watching after the quarterfinals, while your friend watched the finals and knows for a fact that Team B won the championship 00:04:49.

Even though the tournament is over and the outcome is deterministic, we can still represent the winning team $X \in \{A, B, C, D\}$ as a probability distribution $P$ by placing $100\%$ of the probability mass on Team B and $0\%$ on every other team 00:05:33:

$$P = \big[P(A),\, P(B),\, P(C),\, P(D)\big] = \big[0,\, 1,\, 0,\, 0\big]$$

Meanwhile, before the semifinals were played, you might have known that Team B had a strong recent record and predicted a distribution $Q$ that heavily favored Team B while leaving room for an upset 00:06:15.

This exact correspondence powers multi-class classification in deep learning 00:07:42. Imagine classifying photos of four fruits: Apple, Banana, Cherry, and Dragon fruit 00:08:09. When we show the neural network a training image of a banana, the ground-truth label is a one-hot distribution $P = [0, 1, 0, 0]$, and the network's output layer produces a predicted probability distribution $Q$:

Outcome / Class Index Tournament Truth ($P$) Fan Prediction ($Q$) Fruit Image Truth ($P$) Model Softmax Output ($Q$)
Class 1 (Team A / Apple) $0$ $0.20$ $0$ $0.10$
Class 2 (Team B / Banana) ✓ $1$ $0.50$ $1$ $0.65$
Class 3 (Team C / Cherry) $0$ $0.15$ $0$ $0.15$
Class 4 (Team D / Dragon fruit) $0$ $0.15$ $0$ $0.10$

In both settings, training our model boils down to defining a principled loss function $\mathcal{L}(P, Q)$ that measures how far the predicted distribution $Q$ strayed from the true distribution $P$ so we can update the network parameters 00:09:20.

3. Expectations Pair Outcome Probabilities With Outcome Payoffs

Before we introduce information and entropy, we need one mathematical template: expected value 00:10:42.

Suppose sports pundits assign winning probabilities $P = [0.30, 0.40, 0.20, 0.10]$ to teams $A, B, C, D$. You place bets on the tournament such that each outcome $i$ carries a specific rupee gain or loss $v(i)$ 00:11:03:

Winning Team ($i$) Probability $P(X = i)$ Payoff / Reward $v(X = i)$ Weighted Term $P(X = i) \cdot v(X = i)$
Team A $0.30$ ($30\%$) $+10\text{k Rs}$ $0.30 \times 10\text{k} = +3.0\text{k Rs}$
Team B $0.40$ ($40\%$) $+5\text{k Rs}$ $0.40 \times 5\text{k} = +2.0\text{k Rs}$
Team C $0.20$ ($20\%$) $+10\text{k Rs}$ $0.20 \times 10\text{k} = +2.0\text{k Rs}$
Team D $0.10$ ($10\%$) $-30\text{k Rs}$ $0.10 \times (-30\text{k}) = -3.0\text{k Rs}$
Expected Reward $\mathbb{E}[v(X)]$ $1.00$ ($100\%$) — $+4.0\text{k Rs}$
$$\mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}\big[v(X)\big] = \sum_{i \in \{A, B, C, D\}} P(X = i) \cdot v(X = i) = 3\text{k} + 2\text{k} + 2\text{k} - 3\text{k} = 4\text{k Rs}$$
The Mental Template for Everything Ahead: Every expectation combines two ingredients: (1) how often each event happens ($P(X=i)$), and (2) what cost or value gets triggered when that event occurs ($v(X=i)$) 00:12:30. Both Entropy and Cross-Entropy are simply expectations where the rupee payoff $v(X=i)$ is replaced by information content (bits).
Section 04 · 00:13:10 – 00:20:35

4. Information Content Quantifies Surprise Through Negative Logarithms

When someone tells you a piece of news, how much information did you actually gain? 00:13:21

Intuitively, information is synonymous with surprise:

  • Zero surprise ($P = 1 \implies I = 0$): If you walk up to Prof. Mitesh Khapra and tell him, "Your name is Mitesh," he gains zero information because he already knows that with certainty 00:13:50. Likewise, telling someone in a cloudless region that they will see the moon tonight carries almost zero information 00:14:09.
  • High surprise ($P \ll 1 \implies I \gg 0$): Telling you "There will be a lunar eclipse tonight" 00:14:00, or telling a sports fan that the weakest underdog Team D won the tournament instead of powerhouse Team A 00:14:33, delivers a large amount of information because the event had very low prior probability.

This gives us our first requirement: the information content $I(x)$ of an event must be inversely related to its probability $P(x)$ 00:14:55.

Why Must the Function Be a Logarithm?

Knowing that $I(x)$ decreases as $P(x)$ increases isn't enough—we want an exact formula $I(x) = f(P(x))$ 00:15:15. To lock down the function $f$, consider two completely independent random variables 00:15:57:

  1. $X \in \{A, B, C, D\}$: Which team won the tournament outside.
  2. $Y \in \{\text{On}, \text{Off}\}$: Whether the air conditioner in the classroom is currently switched on 00:16:05.

Suppose one person tells you the outcome of $X$ and another tells you the outcome of $Y$. Because the classroom AC has nothing to do with the tournament winner, learning about $X$ gives you zero clue about $Y$. There is no shared overlap to cancel out, so the total information gained from learning both independent facts together must equal the sum of their individual information contents 00:16:53:

$$I(X = x, Y = y) = I(X = x) + I(Y = y)$$

Now rewrite $I(\cdot)$ as our unknown function $f(\cdot)$ of probability 00:18:10. Because $X$ and $Y$ are independent, their joint probability factors into the product of their individual probabilities, $P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x) \cdot P(Y = y)$ 00:18:47:

$$f\big(P(X = x) \cdot P(Y = y)\big) = f\big(P(X = x)\big) + f\big(P(Y = y)\big)$$

What family of functions turns a product inside into a sum outside, $f(a \cdot b) = f(a) + f(b)$? The logarithm! 00:19:10 Combining the logarithm with our inverse-proportionality requirement ($\frac{1}{P}$) gives the canonical formula for information content 00:19:23:

$$I(X = x) = \log\left(\frac{1}{P(X = x)}\right) = -\log P(X = x)$$
Section 05 · 00:20:36 – 00:29:22

5. Entropy Measures Both Expected Surprise and Optimal Bit Length

Once every outcome $i$ of a random variable $X$ has an associated information content $I(X = i) = -\log P(X = i)$, we can ask: What is the expected information content across the entire distribution? 00:21:45

Using our expectation formula from Section 3—weighting each outcome's surprise $-\log P(X = i)$ by its probability $P(X = i)$—defines the Entropy $H(X)$ 00:22:12:

$$H(P) = \mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}\big[-\log P(X)\big] = -\sum_{i} P(X = i) \log P(X = i)$$

Notice what happens for a completely certain event, such as the sun rising in the east ($P = [1, 0]$) 00:22:48: the first term is $-1 \cdot \log(1) = 0$, and the remaining terms are $0 \cdot \log(0) = 0$ (by the limit $\lim_{p \to 0^+} p \log p = 0$), yielding $H(P) = 0$.

Why Bits? The Digital Transmission Connection

Why do we measure entropy in bits when we use $\log_2$? Suppose you want to transmit one of four commands $\{A, B, C, D\}$ over a digital wire 00:23:55:

  • 4 equally likely commands ($P_i = \frac{1}{4}$): We encode them with 2-bit strings (`00`, `01`, `10`, `11`). Notice that $-\log_2\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) = -\log_2(2^{-2}) = 2\text{ bits}$ 00:25:06.
  • 8 equally likely commands ($P_i = \frac{1}{8}$): We encode them with 3-bit strings (`000` through `111`), matching $-\log_2\left(\frac{1}{8}\right) = -\log_2(2^{-3}) = 3\text{ bits}$ 00:25:45.

Now suppose our four commands $\{A, B, C, D\}$ are not equally likely, but instead follow a skewed distribution 00:26:44:

$$P = \left[P(A) = \frac{1}{2},\; P(B) = \frac{1}{4},\; P(C) = \frac{1}{8},\; P(D) = \frac{1}{8}\right]$$

If command $A$ is sent $50\%$ of the time, spending 2 bits on every $A$ wastes bandwidth. Instead, we assign each symbol $i$ a prefix-free binary codeword of length exactly equal to its information content $L_i = -\log_2 P_i$ 00:27:12:

Message ($i$) True Probability ($P_i$) Information Content ($-\log_2 P_i$) Binary Code Expected Bit Usage ($P_i \times L_i$)
Command A $1/2 = 0.500$ $1\text{ bit}$ 0 $0.500 \times 1 = 0.500\text{ bits}$
Command B $1/4 = 0.250$ $2\text{ bits}$ 10 $0.250 \times 2 = 0.500\text{ bits}$
Command C $1/8 = 0.125$ $3\text{ bits}$ 110 $0.125 \times 3 = 0.375\text{ bits}$
Command D $1/8 = 0.125$ $3\text{ bits}$ 111 $0.125 \times 3 = 0.375\text{ bits}$
Total / Entropy $H(P)$ $1.000$ — — $1.750\text{ bits / message}$

By matching our code lengths to $-\log_2 P_i$, our expected cost drops from $2.00\text{ bits}$ down to the entropy $H(P) = 1.75\text{ bits}$—saving $0.25\text{ bits}$ per message on average 00:28:14.

Section 06 · 00:29:23 – 00:35:30

6. Cross-Entropy Captures the Cost of Encoding Reality With an Imperfect Model

Now let's add the crucial real-world wrinkle 00:29:27. In practice, messages are generated by nature according to the true distribution $P = [P_1, P_2, P_3, P_4]$, but we do not know $P$.

Just like our friend peeking into the glass urn, we only observe a finite sample of messages—or use domain priors, such as knowing that a "STOP" command is rarely transmitted except at the very end of a program 00:30:28—to build an estimated distribution $Q = [Q_1, Q_2, Q_3, Q_4]$.

Because we only know $Q$, we design our binary code lengths around $Q$, reserving $L_i = -\log_2 Q_i$ bits for the $i$-th message 00:31:22. Look at the mismatch that creates 00:31:57:

  • Bit cost per symbol (from our estimate $Q$): Symbol $i$ costs $-\log Q_i$ bits every time it is sent.
  • Arrival frequency of each symbol (from reality $P$): Symbol $i$ actually arrives with probability $P_i$ 00:32:38.

Therefore, the expected number of bits we actually spend per transmitted message is the expectation under $P$ of the code lengths generated by $Q$ 00:33:52:

$$H(P, Q) = \mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}\big[-\log Q(X)\big] = -\sum_{i} P_i \log Q_i$$

Section 07 · 00:35:31 – 00:43:50

7. Constrained Optimization Proves Cross-Entropy Is Minimized Only When $P = Q$

Why does Cross-Entropy $H(P, Q) = -\sum_{i=1}^k P_i \log Q_i$ work as a loss function for training machine learning models? Unlike squared error, its minimum value isn't necessarily zero—its minimum is the true entropy $H(P)$, achieved when our model's predicted distribution $Q$ matches the true distribution $P$ 00:35:02.

Wait—how do we know for sure that no other valid distribution $Q$ can produce a cross-entropy even lower than $H(P)$? Let's prove it using calculus 00:36:25.

Step 1: Why Naive Unconstrained Differentiation Fails

Suppose we write out $H(P, Q) = -\left(P_1 \log Q_1 + P_2 \log Q_2 + \dots + P_k \log Q_k\right)$ and naively take the partial derivative with respect to $Q_2$, setting it to zero 00:37:50:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial Q_2}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{k} P_i \log Q_i\right) = -\frac{P_2}{Q_2} = 0$$

This equation gives us nothing useful ($Q_2 \to \infty$)! Why? Because we forgot that $Q$ is constrained to lie on the probability simplex 00:38:37:

$$\min_{Q_1, \dots, Q_k} \left(-\sum_{i=1}^{k} P_i \log Q_i\right) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{k} Q_i = 1$$

Step 2: Solving With a Lagrange Multiplier

To incorporate the constraint $\sum_{i=1}^k Q_i - 1 = 0$, we introduce a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ and form the augmented objective function $\mathcal{J}(Q, \lambda)$ 00:39:11:

$$\mathcal{J}(Q_1, \dots, Q_k, \lambda) = -\sum_{i=1}^{k} P_i \log Q_i + \lambda \left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} Q_i - 1\right)$$

Now take the partial derivative with respect to any coordinate $Q_i$ and set it equal to zero 00:41:49:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{J}}{\partial Q_i} = -\frac{P_i}{Q_i} + \lambda = 0 \implies P_i = \lambda Q_i$$

Since $P_i = \lambda Q_i$ holds for every $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\}$, we can sum both sides across all $k$ outcomes 00:42:35:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k} P_i = \lambda \sum_{i=1}^{k} Q_i \implies 1 = \lambda \cdot 1 \implies \lambda = 1$$

Substituting $\lambda = 1$ back into $P_i = \lambda Q_i$ immediately proves that the unique minimizer satisfies 00:42:45:

$$Q_i = P_i \quad \forall\, i \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\}$$
Section 08 · 00:43:10 – 00:44:28

8. Summary: From Surprise to Classification Loss

Quantity Formula Information & Machine Learning Meaning
Information Content $I(x) = -\log P(x)$ Surprise of observing single outcome $x$; ideal code length in bits.
Entropy $H(P) = -\sum_{i} P_i \log P_i$ Expected surprise under $P$; theoretical minimum average bits per message.
Cross-Entropy $H(P, Q) = -\sum_{i} P_i \log Q_i$ Expected bits used when nature follows $P$ but code lengths are designed from $Q$.
One-Hot Classification Loss $\mathcal{L}(P, Q) = -\log Q_c$ When true class $c$ has $P_c = 1$ and $P_{j \neq c} = 0$, minimizing $H(P, Q)$ maximizes $Q_c \to 1$.

In every classification problem—where the ground truth $P$ places all its mass on the true class $c$ ($P_c = 1$) while our neural network predicts a distribution $Q$ across all classes—minimizing the cross-entropy loss $H(P, Q) = -\sum_i P_i \log Q_i = -\log Q_c$ pushes our model's predicted distribution $Q$ directly toward the true distribution $P$ 00:43:20.
